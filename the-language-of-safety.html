The Language of Safety: Why Policies Alone Don't Protect Children

By Hilda Grace Nalwanga · Aug 1, 2026

Every institution I have audited has a story about the policy that was never read. It sits in a binder on a shelf, or in a folder no one opens, written in language that assumes everyone already knows what it means. Meanwhile, the very people it was written to protect — children — have no idea it exists at all.

Uganda's own Violence Against Children Survey found that three in four young adults experienced some form of violence during childhood. That is not a statistic about institutions with no policies. In most cases, a policy existed somewhere. What was missing was a shared, everyday language of safety — one that staff could actually use, and one that children could actually understand.

A safeguarding policy that lives only in formal, legal language protects an institution's paperwork more than it protects a child. A policy becomes real the moment a teacher knows exactly which sentence to say to a frightened student, the moment a volunteer knows exactly who to call, the moment a child themselves has heard, in words they understand, that they are allowed to tell someone if something feels wrong.

This is why safeguarding audits cannot stop at reviewing documents. When we sit with an organization's staff, we are not just checking whether a reporting procedure exists on paper — we are asking whether a newly hired staff member, three weeks into the job, could describe it in their own words. If they cannot, the policy is not yet doing its job, however well it reads.

The same is true at home. Parents often ask us how to talk to their children about safety without frightening them. The answer is rarely a single big conversation. It is small, repeated, ordinary language woven into everyday life: naming body parts correctly, practicing what to say if someone asks them to keep an uncomfortable secret, and simply telling a child, often, that they will always be believed.

Safeguarding, done well, is not a binder. It is a shared vocabulary that a child, a teacher, a parent, and a board member can all speak fluently — the same words, understood the same way, ready the moment they are needed. That is the standard we hold every institution we work with to, and it is the standard every child deserves.
