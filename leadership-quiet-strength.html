The Quiet Strength of a Leader Who Listens

By Hilda Grace Nalwanga · Aug 4, 2026

I once sat in a boardroom where the loudest voice in the room was mistaken for the strongest leader. He spoke first, spoke longest, and rarely paused to ask what anyone else thought. It took a crisis, months later, for the room to discover that the quietest person at that table had seen the problem coming the whole time — and had simply never been asked.

We tend to picture leadership as decisiveness, as certainty, as the person with the answer. But in every organization I have worked with — schools, churches, NGOs, family businesses — the leaders who build the healthiest cultures are rarely the ones with the most answers. They are the ones who ask the best questions, and then have the discipline to actually wait for the response.

This is not a soft skill tacked onto the edges of leadership. The World Economic Forum consistently names emotional intelligence, adaptability, and people management among the most critical skills for the future of work — not as complements to strategic thinking, but as prerequisites for it. A leader who cannot listen cannot accurately diagnose what is actually happening in their organization, no matter how sharp their strategy looks on paper.

I think often of the difference between managing tasks and leading people. Managing a task asks, "Is it done?" Leading a person asks, "What do you need in order to do this well, and to keep doing it well next year?" The second question takes longer to ask and longer still to answer. It also happens to be the question that determines whether your best people stay.

In our Leadership and Organizational Excellence work, we see this play out again and again: teams do not leave organizations because the mission is unclear. They leave because they stopped feeling heard. Governance structures, strategic plans, and executive coaching all matter — but none of them substitute for a leader willing to sit in silence long enough to actually hear what their people are trying to tell them.

If you lead a team, a school, a ministry, or a family business, here is a simple practice to try this week: in your next three meetings, be the last person to speak, not the first. Notice what surfaces when you are not the one filling the silence. Quiet strength is still strength — it is simply strength that has learned to make room for other voices before it makes up its mind.
